Day 2 — Pathogen evolution, between and within hosts

Wednesday 2 December 2026 · 09:00–12:00

Learning outcomes

  • Derive when a mutant strain can invade a resident strain.
  • Explain why evolution need not maximise transmission (the virulence trade-off).
  • Write down a within-host model of virus, target cells and drug.
  • Explain the mutant selection window: why intermediate drug doses can be worst.

09:00 · Lecture 3

Between hosts: which strain wins?

  • Two strains with complete cross-immunity, in a population with births and deaths.
  • A rare mutant grows when \(\beta_m S^* > \gamma_m + \mu\). At the resident equilibrium \(S^* = 1/R_0^{\text{res}}\), so the strain with the larger \(R_0\) wins, even if it transmits less per contact.
  • Virulence trade-off: if \(\beta(\alpha) \propto \sqrt{\alpha}\), then \(R_0\) is maximised at \(\alpha^* = \gamma + \mu\), an intermediate virulence.
  • A mutant strain arising during one epidemic must appear early, while \(S\) is still high (branching process again).

Within a host: virus, cells and drugs

  • Target-cell model (Perelson 2002): \(T \to I \to V\), with a within-host \(R_0 = \beta \lambda p / (d\,\delta\,c)\).
  • The drug blocks infection with efficacy \(\varepsilon\). The wild type is suppressed if \(\varepsilon > 1 - 1/R_0\).
  • A resistant mutant pays a fitness cost but tolerates the drug better. Three simple dose thresholds follow: where resistance becomes favoured (\(D_x\)), where the wild type alone is stopped (\(D_W\)), and where everything is stopped (\(D_M\)). Between \(D_W\) and \(D_M\) lies the classical mutant selection window.
  • Link to Day 1: whether resistance actually appears is a rescue problem. Deterministic models get this wrong.

## 09:00 · Lecture 3 {.block-lecture}

**Between hosts: which strain wins?**

- Two strains with complete cross-immunity, in a population with births and deaths.
- A rare mutant grows when $\beta_m S^* > \gamma_m + \mu$. At the resident equilibrium $S^* = 1/R_0^{\text{res}}$, so **the strain with the larger $R_0$ wins**, even if it transmits less per contact.
- Virulence trade-off: if $\beta(\alpha) \propto \sqrt{\alpha}$, then $R_0$ is maximised at $\alpha^* = \gamma + \mu$, an intermediate virulence.
- A mutant strain arising *during* one epidemic must appear early, while $S$ is still high (branching process again).

**Within a host: virus, cells and drugs**

- Target-cell model (Perelson 2002): $T \to I \to V$, with a within-host $R_0 = \beta \lambda p / (d\,\delta\,c)$.
- The drug blocks infection with efficacy $\varepsilon$. The wild type is suppressed if $\varepsilon > 1 - 1/R_0$.
- A resistant mutant pays a fitness cost but tolerates the drug better. Three simple dose thresholds follow: where resistance becomes favoured ($D_x$), where the wild type alone is stopped ($D_W$), and where everything is stopped ($D_M$). Between $D_W$ and $D_M$ lies the classical **mutant selection window**.
- Link to Day 1: whether resistance actually appears is a rescue problem. Deterministic models get this wrong.
